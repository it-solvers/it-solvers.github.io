Finance Lease vs. Operating Lease: Revenue Recognition and Journal Entries

In a lease (a contract in which you let someone use an asset and receive payments for it), the lessor receives the same cash either way. The difference between the two lease types is how that cash is recorded in the books (the journal entry). We follow a 36-month example with monthly payments.

How amounts are calculatedEvery amount on this page is a value truncated (rounded down) to the nearest $1. This applies to the monthly lease payment, interest, principal, and depreciation. All figures are in US dollars.

Looking at the lessor’s books

This page explains the lessor’s accounting (the company that leases out the asset). Accounting is the set of rules for recording transactions, and a lessor’s books have two main parts.

  • Statement of financial position (balance sheet: shows what the company owns and owes): for a finance lease, a receivable (the net investment in the lease) is recorded; for an operating lease, the vehicle stays on the books as an asset.
  • Statement of profit or loss (income statement: shows income earned and costs incurred): a finance lease shows finance income; an operating lease shows lease income and depreciation.

Tax rules can differ from accounting rules and are not covered on this page.

Example assumptions

  • The lessor buys a $100,000 vehicle and leases it to a customer for 36 months.
  • Annual interest rate of 12% (1% per month), with level payments (the same amount every month, covering both interest and principal).
  • Monthly lease payment = $3,321, so 36 payments total $119,556. Total profit is $19,556.
  • All amounts are truncated (rounded down). The monthly lease payment, monthly interest, principal, and depreciation are all calculated in $1 units. In the last payment, any remaining balance is cleared so that the totals match.
  • To keep things simple, deposits, advance payments, and residual value (the value left in the vehicle when the contract ends) are all set to zero.
  • Operating lease depreciation is assumed to use the 48-month (4-year) straight-line method (the same amount is expensed every month). Depreciating over a period longer than the lease term (36 months) reflects that lessors often depreciate over about 4 years; the depreciation period varies by lessor. As a result, the vehicle still has a carrying amount at the end of payment 36.
How to read a journal entry A journal entry records a transaction by splitting it into a debit (the left side of the ledger) and a credit (the right side of the ledger). Increases in assets and expenses go on the debit side; decreases in assets and income go on the credit side. Total debits always equal total credits. Check that the totals match in each table below.

Advanced 1

The shape of operating lease profit changes with the depreciation method

The basic example showed only the straight-line method. Here we compare three ways of spreading depreciation for an operating lease: straight-line, diminishing balance, and the principal-matching method (원금상환비례법; a descriptive label, not a standard term). Changing the method changes how profit is split across years. This section uses a 15% annual rate, so the rate and the numbers differ from the basic example (12%).

Advanced example assumptions

  • Cost of $100,000, 15% annual rate, 36-month lease, monthly lease payment $3,466 (36 payments total $124,776), total profit over the lease term of $24,776
  • Straight-line and diminishing balance are calculated over 4 years (48 months), which is common in practice. Lessors often depreciate over about 4 years, and the useful life (the depreciation period) varies by lessor. This is the same 48 months as the straight-line method in the basic example (only the interest rate differs).
  • The diminishing balance method uses a depreciation rate of 0.528 for a 4-year life. The depreciation rate varies by lessor. 0.528 means 52.8% per year; the monthly depreciation is that rate divided by 12, multiplied by the carrying amount on the first day of the year.
  • The principal-matching method uses the lease term (36 months). That is because the principal repaid under the finance lease is used directly as depreciation (this method has no standard English name; “principal-matching” is a descriptive label). We also assume no accrued-income adjustment for operating lease payments: income that is earned but not yet received is not calculated separately, and each month’s lease payment is recorded as that month’s income as is.
  • When the lease ends, the vehicle is sold and settled; for illustration, the sale price is assumed to be $30,000.
  • Each payment is compared with the same payment number: payment 1 profit with payment 1 interest, payment 2 profit with payment 2 interest, and so on.
Finance lease

Only interest is profit. Shown as a baseline for comparison.

Operating lease · Straight-line

Depreciation is the same amount every month for 48 months.

Operating lease · Diminishing balance

The annual rate of 0.528 (52.8%) divided by 12 is applied to the carrying amount on the first day of the year. The amount is the same each month within a year, and is recalculated on the reduced carrying amount when the year changes. So depreciation is large at first and then declines.

Operating lease · Principal-matching method

Depreciation is set equal to the principal repaid under the finance lease. Profit is then exactly the same as the finance lease every month.

Carrying amount on the first day of next year = carrying amount on the first day of this year − (monthly depreciation × 12). For example, the first day of year 2 is $100,000 − $4,400 × 12 = $47,200. In year 1, depreciation is larger than lease income, so every month is a loss; from year 2 it turns into profit.

Where does 0.528 come from?

The diminishing balance depreciation rate is generally said to be calculated as 1 − (residual value ÷ cost)1 ÷ useful life, where residual value is the value left after use. If the residual value is set to 5% of cost and the useful life to 4 years, the rate is 1 − 0.051/4 = 1 − 0.473 = about 0.527, and rounding up at the third decimal place gives 0.528.

This page uses the rounded-up result above as the depreciation rate. The residual value ratio and the depreciation rate vary by lessor.

After the lease ends: the remaining carrying amount is settled as a gain or loss on derecognition

Straight-line and diminishing balance spread depreciation over 48 months, so a carrying amount remains on the vehicle even when the lease ends at payment 36. This is not an error. When the vehicle is sold, the difference between the sale price and the carrying amount is recorded as a gain or loss on derecognition (the profit or loss that arises when an asset is sold).

The depreciation method changes only when profit is recognized. The diminishing balance method depreciates more early, leaving a smaller carrying amount and a larger gain on sale; straight-line leaves a larger carrying amount and a smaller gain.

What the accounting standard says (Korean Lease Accounting Practice Standard, Section 6-2) In short: when a lessor depreciates operating lease assets using the diminishing balance method or similar, depreciation can exceed lease income early in the lease term and cause a loss; the standard allows recognizing income so that the profit from the asset is even across periods. Journal entry when the vehicle is sold after the lease ends

When the 36-month lease ends, the lessor sells the returned vehicle as a used car. A journal entry is needed to remove the vehicle from the books. Follow the steps below and it is not hard.

Two terms first

  • Accumulated depreciation: the running total of the monthly depreciation expense. For example, if you depreciated $2,083 a month for 36 months, accumulated depreciation is $2,083 × 36 = $74,988. The vehicle asset itself stays at $100,000, and the amount written down is accumulated separately in this account.
  • Carrying amount: the current value of the vehicle as recorded in the books (also called book value). carrying amount = vehicle cost − accumulated depreciation; for straight-line, $100,000 − $74,988 = $25,012.

3 things to do when the vehicle is sold

  1. Record the money received. The vehicle sold for $30,000 in cash, so debit Cash.
  2. Remove the vehicle from the books. The vehicle no longer belongs to the company, so credit the vehicle cost of $100,000 to remove it, and debit accumulated depreciation to clear it. Both accounts go to $0.
  3. Record the remaining difference as a gain or loss on derecognition. If you record only steps 1 and 2, debits and credits do not balance. The difference is exactly the sale price minus the carrying amount, and recording it as a gain (or a loss, if negative) balances the entry.
gain on derecognition = sale price − carrying amount
Straight-line: $30,000 − $25,012 = $4,988

Put simply, the books said “this car is worth about $25,012,” and you sold it for $30,000, so you gained the difference. The larger the carrying amount (the less you have written down), the smaller the gain.

Even if you sell the same car at the same price, the accumulated depreciation differs by depreciation method, so the carrying amount and the gain differ.
